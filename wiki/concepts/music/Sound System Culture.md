# Sound System Culture

[[Sound System Culture]] refers to the practice of assembling large, powerful speaker systems — typically outdoors or in warehouses and dance halls — to play recorded music for communal dancing. Though it has spread globally and mutated into countless forms, its roots lie firmly in Jamaica in the 1950s and 1960s, where operators would compete with each other through the power of their rigs, the exclusivity of their records, and the skill of their selectors (DJs) and MCs.

The [[Dub-Reggae]] tradition is inseparable from sound system culture. Producers like King Tubby and Lee "Scratch" Perry developed dub as a form specifically designed to showcase bass response through large speaker arrays — stripping out or manipulating elements of existing reggae recordings to create versions that foregrounded low frequencies, echo, and space. The result was music built _for_ a physical environment and a collective body, not for headphones or radio.

As the Jamaican diaspora moved to cities like London, New York, and Toronto through the 1960s and 70s, sound system culture traveled with it. In the UK, this transplanted tradition became foundational to what would eventually emerge as [[Jungle / Drum and Bass]], [[Dancehall]], [[Hardcore]], and the broader [[Hardcore Continuum]] theorized by music critics and scholars. In the US, it fed into [[Hip-Hop]] block party culture and [[Miami Bass]]. The academic conference described in Source 1 explicitly frames sound system culture as the origin point for most of what we now call [[Bass Culture]].

What makes sound system culture theoretically rich — and why it attracts the interest of scholars working in [[Sonic Materialism]] and [[Sonic Epistemology]] — is that it treats sound not as representation but as _force_. The bass frequencies produced by a sound system are not just heard; they are felt in the chest, the gut, the feet. This blurs the line between listening and bodily experience, between music and environment. The dance hall becomes an instrument.

Today, [[Sound System Culture]] persists in scenes as varied as [[Psytrance]] outdoor festivals, [[Footwork]] events in Chicago, carnival sound system competitions in the UK, and the global underground scenes studied by the [[Sonic Street Technologies]] research project. Though the technology has changed — digital files replacing [[Vinyl and Dubplates]], computer-controlled amplification replacing hand-built rigs — the core logic remains: gather people, move air, move bodies.

Understanding [[Sound System Culture]] is arguably the single most important prerequisite for understanding why bass matters — not just sonically, but socially and politically.
