## Part I — Research: What Actually Works for Music Culture Accounts on X

### The landscape

The rave/electronic music community on X is small, skeptical, and high-value. It includes DJs, promoters, music journalists (Resident Advisor, NTS, Mixmag staff and freelancers), label managers, record shop owners, and dedicated collectors. This is not a TikTok audience. They are not there for content. They are there for conversation, provocation, and signal.

The accounts that break through in this space share a few traits:

**They participate before they broadcast.** The accounts with real credibility in the UK music/rave space built it by being consistently smart in *other people's* threads. Replying to a Ben UFO tweet with something genuinely interesting is worth more than a hundred original posts. Reputation precedes followers here.

**They have a specific angle, not a general topic.** "Electronic music" is too broad to build authority. The accounts that work are narrowly positioned: one person who deep-dives on UK Garage, one who posts Jungle breakdowns, one who photographs flyer archives. Specificity signals depth.

**They use threads strategically, not habitually.** A well-constructed thread once a week — "five things I found researching the Criminal Justice Act that didn't make it into the script" — outperforms daily single tweets. The music history audience saves and shares long-form threads. Bookmark rate is the real metric.

**They post archival material.** Flyers, fanzine scans, vintage photos, label press sheets. These posts reliably attract the established figures in the scene because they trigger nostalgia and knowledge-sharing. A flyer from a 1992 Amnesia House night gets real commentary from people who were there. That commentary is the content.

**They ask the right questions.** Not "what's your favourite rave track?" (too broad, too obvious). Better: "What's the one record that told you exactly what kind of DJ you weren't going to be?" Specific, unexpected, requires actual thought. The right question pulls in the right people.

### Content formats that work

| Format | What it does |
|---|---|
| Research thread (3–6 tweets) | Demonstrates depth; high save rate; shareable to people outside the account's followers |
| Archival image + one-line context | Triggers nostalgia and expert responses; discovery surface |
| Hot take, briefly defended | Creates replies; should be a real opinion, not manufactured controversy |
| Quote-tweet with added value | Gets noticed by the original poster; compounds with their audience |
| Question that requires expertise to answer | Filters in the right people; builds comments as community lore |
| Episode clip + the one thing the clip doesn't tell you | Drives to the series while adding a reason to engage here too |

### What doesn't work

- Scheduled broadcast content with no personality
- Posting links with no framing ("new episode out now")
- Blanket hashtagging (the music community on X doesn't search hashtags; they follow accounts)
- Performing enthusiasm ("SO excited to share this!!")
- Asking for follows or engagement directly

### Posting cadence that works for niche music accounts

**Frequency:** 3–5 posts/week. Consistency matters more than volume. Missing a week hurts; daily posting for its own sake hurts too.

**Timing:** UK-centric music accounts are most active weekday evenings (18:00–22:00 GMT) and Friday–Saturday. Japanese audience is JST afternoon/evening. Best window: 18:00–20:00 GMT (= 03:00–05:00 JST) is peak UK time — post during UK prime hours to catch the tastemakers, not the casual viewers.

**The asymmetry:** 80% replies and engagement with others, 20% original posts. This is the X-specific rule that most creators ignore. It's backwards from TikTok logic.

### Growth tactics specific to rave/music community on X

1. **The reply game.** Identify 15–20 accounts in the UK rave/music space (journalists, DJs, promoters, label accounts). Read them every day. Reply when you have something genuine to add. Not "great point" — actual content. Do this for three months before expecting anything back.

2. **The quote-tweet with context.** When a music story breaks (a club closure, a label milestone, a historical anniversary), be the first account to add a specific, well-researched contextual angle. This is how you get noticed by people who don't follow you yet.

3. **Tagging with intention.** When an episode mentions a specific artist or figure, one careful, respectful tag on the episode announcement is appropriate. Not every episode. Not every person mentioned. The ones most likely to engage — and most credible as a signal of legitimacy.

4. **The "what didn't make it in" thread.** Every episode has 60–70% of the research on the cutting room floor. That material is gold on X. A thread posted 2–3 days after an episode drops — "things I researched for EP-04 that didn't fit the script" — drives delayed discovery of the episode and gives people who already watched it a reason to engage again.

5. **Flyer Friday / Archive post.** One archival find per week, with a one-sentence context note. No heavy production required. These posts attract comments from people who were there, which builds community lore and signals to newcomers that the account is trusted by people with real knowledge.

6. **Be findable in a niche moment.** When FABRIC announces something, when a legendary DJ retires, when a new documentary drops — be in that conversation with something substantive. This is ambient discoverability, not manufactured virality.

**Pinned Tweet Concept:**

A thread opener that functions as a permanent introduction:

> "I'm Japanese. I've never been to a UK rave. I'm making a 20-episode video series about UK rave culture history.
>
> Here's why a Japanese DJ became obsessed with a scene she's never been inside — and what I'm actually trying to understand ↓"
>

The goal: someone who discovers RINΘ via a reply or quote-tweet clicks the profile, reads this, and understands the whole story in 90 seconds.

**Content Pillars:**

1. **Research fragments** — "Researching EP-08 and found something I didn't expect..." posts that tease depth without requiring the episode. These posts should feel like dispatches from someone actually doing the work, not a content schedule.

2. **Hot takes with receipts** — Real opinions on rave culture, DJ culture, music education. Not manufactured controversy. Example: "The 'rave was utopian' narrative is real and also completely misses who didn't get to be there. EP-06 goes into this." One take, briefly defended, with a hook back to the work.

3. **Japan / UK cultural bridges** — The specific lens only RINΘ has. What does rave culture look like from a Japanese perspective? What's lost in translation and what travels perfectly? These posts are unique and attract people from both communities.

4. **Pre-move / arrival content** — UK visa prep, flat hunting in London, first time at a UK record shop, first UK club night. This is the ongoing life narrative that makes the account a person, not a channel.

**Posting Cadence:**
- 3–4 posts/week
- No fixed schedule — reactive and timely is more credible than clockwork here
- 80/20 rule: most interaction should be replies to others, not original posts
- Episode weeks: one post tied to the episode (clip + thread or the "what didn't make it in" follow-up)

**Growth Tactics:**

- Build the reply game before launch: spend 4 weeks engaging with UK music/rave accounts before posting any original content
- Use the Japan angle as a differentiator in replies — a Japanese perspective on UK rave history is genuinely interesting to the UK side of this community
- Tag selectively and authentically: if a DJ mentioned in an episode is active on X, one respectful tag on the episode announcement post
- Maintain a list of 20 target accounts (music journalists, DJs, promoters, record stores) — read and engage with them weekly
- Never perform enthusiasm. The community respects restraint and specificity

**Accounts to engage with (target list to build):**
Resident Advisor, The Wire Magazine, FACT Magazine, Mixmag, NTS Radio, specific DJs active on X in the UK rave/bass music space, independent UK promoters, Rough Trade / Phonica / HMV Vault accounts.

2. **Research threads** — Mid-week: "things we found that didn't make it into EP-X." These post 2–3 days after each episode drops, when initial watch traffic is fading. They re-surface the episode and give the engaged audience something extra.

- Tuesday: episode drop post

- Archive posts should have correct tagging for discovery (not hashtags — quote-retweets from music history accounts, correct @ tags for organisations)
- The research thread format is the primary growth lever: it's shareable by people outside the existing follower base who care about the specific topic

## Part III — Threads Account Design

*Threads functions differently from X. The algorithm rewards earnest, personal writing over hot takes. The audience skews toward people who consume creative and cultural content on Instagram — they came from Reels, not from journalism. The tone should be warmer, more personal, less armored.*

---

### Account 1: RINΘ (personal, Threads)

**Bio (max 160 chars):**
```
Japanese DJ in love with UK rave culture from afar. Moving to London. Making a video series about the music that built me. Writing to understand it.
```
*(149 chars)*

**Pinned Post Concept:**

A personal essay-style post (Threads allows long text):

> "I grew up listening to music I didn't fully understand.
>
> D&B from the 90s. Old jungle. The kind of records that felt like they came from a world I'd never be allowed into. I'm Japanese. That world was a basement in Leeds in 1992.
>
> I've been researching that world for two years. I'm making a video series about it. And I'm moving to London to see it in person.
>
> This account is where I figure it out out loud. If you're into it, welcome."

This is the entry point for anyone who finds the Threads account. It's honest, it's specific, it creates an arc.

**Content Pillars:**

1. **Research diary entries** — "What I found this week" format. Not structured thread, not a list — a paragraph or two, written conversationally, about one thing that surprised or moved Rinka while researching an episode. This is the format that builds an audience on Threads.

2. **Music discovery posts** — "I didn't know this record existed until last Tuesday. Here's what it taught me." Personal listening notes with cultural context. This is where the Japanese perspective on UK music lands naturally — the gap between hearing it from afar and understanding it from inside.

3. **Pre-move Japan → UK posts** — Practical and emotional. Visa paperwork, record shops visited before leaving Japan, music that's playing on the moving day playlist. This is the personal narrative that connects casual followers to the longer arc of the project.

4. **Moments of genuine uncertainty** — "I don't know if I understand PLUR well enough to explain it yet." These posts perform unusually well on Threads because the platform rewards authenticity over performance. Admitting complexity is a feature, not a weakness.

**Posting Cadence:**
- 3 posts/week
- Longer than X — aim for 3–6 sentences per post minimum, occasionally longer essays
- No scheduling rigidity — the best Threads posts read like they came from a moment of genuine feeling or discovery
- Cross-post to Instagram Stories when a post lands particularly well (the accounts are linked)

**Growth Tactics:**

- **Earnestness as strategy.** The Threads algorithm rewards posts with high reply rates, and the fastest way to get replies is to write something that feels genuinely human. Avoid polished brand voice entirely.
- **Engage with music and culture accounts that are building authentic communities on Threads** — music journalists who moved from Twitter, DJs who are active on the platform, culture writers.
- **The serialisation hook.** Every few weeks, a post that says: "I've been making a video series about rave culture. If you want to follow the arc — Episode 1 is [X], Episode 6 is [Y], and I'm currently on Episode 12." This lets new followers onboard without requiring them to go back through everything.
- **The Japan angle, again, differently.** Threads has a strong Japanese creator community. Content that bridges Japanese culture and Western music scenes has a cross-cultural discoverability advantage that doesn't exist on X.
- **Comments as conversation, not broadcast.** Reply to every comment in the first 24 hours. The Threads algorithm rewards comment depth; replying creates nested threads that push the original post to more people.

### Account 2: @diveintotheraver (series, Threads)

**Bio (max 160 chars):**
```
A video series about rave culture. We start from scratch: what is a rave, why does it matter, what happened to it. New episode every Tuesday. EN/JA.
```
*(149 chars)*

**Pinned Post Concept:**

A series overview written as a personal recommendation:

> "If you've ever wondered where electronic music comes from — not who invented it, but *why it exists*, what it was trying to do, who was in those rooms and what they were feeling — this series is for that.
>
> 20 episodes. Starts at zero. No assumed knowledge.
>
> We're a Japanese creator making this in both English and Japanese, because this history belongs to anyone who cares about it.
>
> Latest episode: [Title]. Follow along."

**Content Pillars:**

1. **Episode announcements** — Softer than X. Not "new episode out now" but a short personal note about why this episode matters. One or two sentences that create feeling before they create clicks.

2. **Extended episode context** — The Threads equivalent of the research thread: a paragraph (not a list) expanding on something from the episode. Written in the same warm, curious tone as the episode itself. This is what drives saves and shares on Threads.

3. **Community questions** — "What's the first electronic music record you heard that you couldn't explain?" These work on Threads because the audience is less defensive than X — they're more willing to share a genuine memory publicly.

4. **Behind-the-series posts** — How the bilingual production works. What it looks like to research 1992 UK rave history from Japan in 2026. These humanise the series and distinguish it from algorithmic content farms. Authenticity is the production value on Threads.

**Posting Cadence:**
- Tuesday: episode announcement (personal framing, not broadcast)
- Thursday or Friday: extended context post or community question
- Total: 2 posts/week — this account is supplementary to RINΘ personal account, not a parallel feed

**Growth Tactics:**

- **Cross-link the accounts explicitly.** Threads users follow people, not brands — make RINΘ personal account the primary, @diveintotheraver the secondary. Posts from the series account should feel like they were written by a person (because they are).
- **Use Threads' Instagram integration.** Sharing series posts to Instagram Stories is native and free. Every Threads post from @diveintotheraver should be shared to the Instagram Reels account's Story on the same day.
- **The comment section as community.** Actively respond to every comment. Pin replies that add genuine knowledge ("my dad was at that rave" type comments become anchors for the post).
- **Long-form posts outperform short ones here.** Unlike X, Threads rewards writing length. A five-sentence post about why the Criminal Justice Act mattered will outperform a one-sentence post every time.
- **Don't replicate the X account.** The content should feel like a different room in the same building — same project, different energy. X is where you talk to the scene; Threads is where you invite people into it.
