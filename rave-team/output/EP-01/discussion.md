# EP-01 DJ × Producer Discussion
## テーマ: Raveとは何か

---

**PRODUCER → DJ**

Research is in. Topic is EP-01 — "Raveとは何か." Japanese audience, many of whom know clubs but not raves. We need to land *what it actually was* before the history episodes hit. Read the brief and tell me what we're working with.

---

**DJ**

[DJ TAKE]

The research is accurate. It's also bloodless.

Everything in there is true — the warehouse, the secrecy, the no VIP, the sub-bass in your chest. But it reads like someone describing a swimming pool to a person who's never been in water. The correct words are there. The thing itself isn't.

What's missing is the specific feeling of not knowing where you're going. You're in a car at 1am, you've called the number, you've been given a postcode, and you're driving into darkness with strangers who are also strangers to each other. That's not a logistical detail. That's the whole architecture of trust the night was built on. The party hadn't earned your trust yet — and you went anyway.

Also: the Black and gay Chicago origins are footnoted, not centered. The research mentions it, then moves on. That's a mistake. Every sound in that room in 1988 came from people who needed a space where they couldn't be touched. That's not background — that's the root.

[THE DETAIL THAT MATTERS]

Larry Heard. "Can You Feel It." 1986. Four minutes and two seconds.

That track wasn't made for a rave. It was made for a back room in Chicago for maybe two hundred people who had nowhere else to be safe. And when it crossed the Atlantic and hit those warehouse speakers — systems so large the bass moved your clothes — something unlocked. The music wasn't asking you anything. It wasn't performing. It was just *there*, like a pulse. That's what rave borrowed from house: the idea that music doesn't need to look at you.

[MY CONCERN]

The comparison table — rave vs club. It's useful for an explainer but it flattens the edge. Clubs aren't just the *commercial version* of rave. They're what happened when someone looked at rave and thought: how do we sell this? The table makes it feel like a lifestyle choice. It was a refusal. Subtle difference, but if the script misses it, the whole episode lands wrong.

Also: PLUR. Frankie Bones is in the timeline but his actual famous line is missing — "peace, love, and unity... I'll break your faces." That line matters because it shows PLUR wasn't naïve. It was a hard-won code enforced by someone who'd seen what happens without it. Don't let the scriptwriter treat PLUR as a slogan.

[THE THREAD]

One question that nobody asked at the time: *why did it feel like freedom?*

Not because of drugs. Not only because of that. Because for one night, in a field or a warehouse in the middle of nowhere, you couldn't be found. No address. No name on a list. No dress code sorting you into who you were supposed to be. Thatcher's Britain had told a generation they had no future. The rave said: fine. Then we'll make our own night.

That's the thread. Not "what is a rave" — but "what does it feel like to be somewhere that isn't supposed to exist."

---

**PRODUCER → DJ**

That last line is the episode. "Somewhere that isn't supposed to exist" — that's the frame the scriptwriter carries from the first sentence to the last. We'll anchor on the drive-into-darkness, give the Chicago roots proper weight upfront, and use Frankie Bones' real line to show PLUR had teeth. "Can You Feel It" closes it.
