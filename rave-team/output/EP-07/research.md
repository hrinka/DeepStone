## Key Facts

### Roland TB-303
- Released 1981 by Roland Corporation as a bass guitar simulator / auto-accompaniment device
- Commercial failure: could not convincingly mimic a real bass guitar, difficult to program
- Discontinued 1984 after ~10,000 units sold
- Surplus units sold off cheaply — as low as $50 USD on the secondhand market
- Chicago producers acquired them in the mid-1980s

### Phuture and "Acid Tracks" (1987)
- Phuture = Nathan "DJ Pierre" Jones + Earl "Spanky" Smith Jr. + Herbert "Herb J" Jackson (Chicago)
- Bought a cheap TB-303, didn't know how to operate it correctly
- DJ Pierre quote: "When we made 'Acid Tracks', that was an accident. It was just ignorance, basically. Not knowing how to work the damn 303."
- The squelching, resonant, liquid sound was produced by misuse — manipulating the machine as it played
- "Acid Tracks" released 1987 on Trax Records — widely cited as the first acid house track
- Ron Hardy (Music Box, Chicago) first played it to a crowd — had to play it four times before people responded positively
- The track spread across the Atlantic in 1988

### The "Acid" Name
- Theories: Chicago slang "acid burn" (to steal/copy); reference to acid/LSD; or the acidic, corrosive sonic quality of the 303's sound
- Most likely: the sound itself was described as "acid" by early Chicago DJs

### Smiley Face Origin
- Harvey Ball created the smiley face in 1963 for an insurance company poster (Worcester, Massachusetts) — paid $45, never copyrighted it
- Fashion designer Barnzley Armitage wore smiley face shirts in ~1986 as ironic nod to 1967 Summer of Love
- Danny Rampling encountered Barnzley and was struck: "I picked up on the smiley face logo from a fashion designer called Barnsley. I ran into him one night when he was covered in these smiley face badges and I thought, 'Wow! That's it! The smiley face completely signifies what this movement is all about — big smiles and positivity.'"
- George Georgiou (Shoom's graphic designer) designed the Shoom flyer with tumbling smileys — he was initially resistant but Rampling insisted
- Shoom: opened January 1988 in a Bermondsey gym, south London. Capacity: 240.
- Bomb The Bass (Tim Simenon / Barnzley) put smiley on the sleeve of "Beat Dis" → i-D magazine cover → mass adoption

### The Sun's About-Face
- October 12, 1988: The Sun ran piece calling acid house "cool and groovy" + offered smiley face t-shirts for sale
- October 19, 1988: Same paper ran headline "Evils of Ecstasy" linking acid house to MDMA
- Trigger: death of 21-year-old Janet Mayes after taking two ecstasy tablets in October 1988
- Tony Colston-Hayter (Sunrise parties organizer) labeled "Acid's Mr Big" by tabloids
- Resulted in BBC banning acid house tracks, club/venue crackdowns, retail bans on merchandise

### Acid House in UK Context
- Acid house was the sonic engine of the Second Summer of Love (1988-89)
- Shoom → Heaven → Spectrum → orbital raves (Sunrise, Biology, Energy)
- The 303's squelch became instantly recognizable as the "sound" of the movement
- Acid house faded quickly as a genre by 1989-90 but its DNA lived on in rave, jungle, techno

## Story Angles for EP-07

1. **Failure angle**: Roland's failed product becomes the most influential instrument in electronic music — irony at the core
2. **Ignorance as innovation**: DJ Pierre didn't know how to use the 303. The mistake was the music.
3. **Smiley face as borrowed symbol**: Harvey Ball (insurance company) → Barnzley Armitage (irony) → Danny Rampling (sincere) — same symbol, three completely different contexts
4. **Media flip**: 7 days from "cool and groovy" to "Evils of Ecstasy" — The Sun's schizophrenic coverage as proof that the culture had arrived
